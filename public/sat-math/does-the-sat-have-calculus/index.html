Does the SAT Have Calculus?

No, the SAT does not test calculus. There are no derivatives, no integrals, and no limits anywhere on the exam. If you have not taken a calculus class yet, you are not behind, and if you have taken one, none of it will show up on test day. The math that actually decides your score tops out at advanced algebra and function work, and this page lays out exactly what is tested and what is not.

The short answer

No, the SAT does not test calculus. The exam stops well below it, and that has been true for every version of the current SAT. Students ask this question for a good reason. Calculus has a reputation as the scary top of the high school math ladder, so it feels natural to assume the most important college entrance test would include it. It does not.

The SAT is built to measure the math skills colleges expect from every incoming student, not the advanced coursework that only some students reach. Plenty of test takers sit for the SAT before they ever open a calculus book, so including it would be unfair. Instead, College Board keeps the ceiling at skills nearly every junior has already seen. Everything on the SAT Math section fits inside four domains, and calculus is not one of them.

This holds for the digital SAT as well. The shift to a shorter, adaptive format changed the timing and the delivery, not the math content, so the same four domains carry over and calculus stays off the list. If you were hoping the newer version quietly added it, it did not, and if you were dreading that, you can relax.

What the SAT actually tests

The SAT Math section is organized into four domains, and knowing the rough weight of each one tells you where your points live. The percentages below are approximate and published by College Board, but they are stable enough to plan around. Here is the plain version of each, with a link to a full practice hub.

Algebra is about 35 percent of the section and covers linear equations, functions, systems, and inequalities. This is the backbone of the test, and it rewards clean, careful setup far more than clever tricks.

Advanced Math is another 35 percent and covers quadratics, exponentials, polynomials, radicals, and other nonlinear functions. This is the hardest material on the exam and the closest the SAT comes to a ceiling, which matters for the next section.

Problem Solving and Data Analysis is about 15 percent and covers ratios, percentages, probability, and statistics. These questions lean on reading and interpretation as much as computation, so slowing down to understand the setup pays off.

Geometry and Trigonometry is the last 15 percent and covers area, volume, triangles, circles, and right triangle trigonometry. It is the smallest domain, but one forgotten formula can cost an easy question, so it is worth steady review.

What is NOT on the SAT

To be clear about the top line: calculus is not on the SAT. No derivatives, no integrals, no limits, and no differential equations. Advanced Math is the ceiling, and it stops at nonlinear functions like quadratics, exponentials, and polynomials. The hardest questions you will meet are advanced algebra and function problems, not anything from a calculus course.

A few other topics students expect are also absent. There are no formal proofs, matrices beyond the basics are not required, and work with imaginary or complex numbers is minimal at most. As a rule of thumb, if a topic lives in a typical calculus class or an upper level course, you can safely assume the SAT leaves it out. That is not a loophole, it is the design of the test. Knowing what is off the list saves you from spending weeks on material that will never earn a single point.

So what should you study instead

Here is the useful part. Because calculus is off the table, your prep time has a clear target. Algebra and Advanced Math together make up about 70 percent of the section, so they deserve most of your attention. Getting reliably strong on linear and nonlinear functions will move your score more than anything else you can do. That is where a strong score is built, one skill at a time.

Problem Solving and Data Analysis and Geometry and Trigonometry fill the remaining 30 percent. They are worth steady maintenance so you do not drop easy points, but the big two are where the score is won. The most efficient plan is to drill your weakest skill inside those domains until it stops surprising you, then move to the next one. Questions students ask

Does the SAT have calculus?
No. The SAT does not test calculus. There are no derivatives, integrals, or limits.
What is the highest level of math on the SAT?
Advanced Math, which covers quadratics, exponentials, polynomials, and nonlinear functions. That is the ceiling, not calculus.
Is calculus on the digital SAT?
No. The digital SAT tests the same four domains as before, and none of them include calculus.
What math should I focus on for the SAT?
Algebra and Advanced Math, which together are about 70 percent of the section.
Do I need to take calculus before the SAT?
No. Algebra 2 level skills cover the hardest SAT Math. Calculus is not required or tested.
